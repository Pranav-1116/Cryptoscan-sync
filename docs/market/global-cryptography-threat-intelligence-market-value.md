# Global market value: cryptography threat intelligence

**Document status:** strategic market hypothesis and commercial planning reference
**Date:** 2026-10-02
**Audience:** founders, product, sales, partnerships, finance, investors, and enterprise buyers

> **Short answer:** CryptoScan is not the whole cybersecurity market, and its value
> cannot honestly be stated as a single market-size number or a current company
> valuation. It sits at the intersection of information-security spending,
> cryptography and encryption, post-quantum migration, software and technology
> supply-chain risk, vendor risk, and cyber threat intelligence. The credible
> opportunity is therefore a **wedge into several adjacent budgets**, not the sum
> of every published market report.

This document separates four things that are often incorrectly mixed together:

1. **Reference market spend** — what organizations spend across a broad category.
2. **Addressable product wedge** — the part of that spend that could fund a
   cryptographic intelligence, inventory, migration, and evidence platform.
3. **Customer economic value** — the cost, delay, exposure, and decision
   uncertainty the product may help reduce after customer validation.
4. **CryptoScan company value** — an investment or acquisition valuation, which
   is unknown until there is audited revenue, retention, margins, product proof,
   customer concentration data, and a negotiated transaction.

No number in this document is a CryptoScan valuation, a guaranteed market share,
a guaranteed saving, or a customer outcome.

### Illustrative bottom-up wedge, not a market fact

The following scenario shows how a focused enterprise product could be sized without

These are **illustrative assumptions**, not counts of real organizations, a

pricing experiments, retention data, deployment cost, and procurement evidence
before using the scenario externally. The arithmetic also excludes discounts,
services, churn, taxes, support, sales cost, and implementation cost.

A company valuation cannot be inferred from the $750M or $150M figures. If a
future finance exercise uses an explicitly selected revenue multiple, it should
show the multiple, period, recurring-revenue quality, growth, margins, dilution,
and comparable transactions separately. Until that evidence exists, CryptoScan's
current monetary valuation remains unknown.

## 2. Why the opportunity exists

The commercial problem is not simply that organizations need another scanner.
The problem is that cryptography is distributed across:

- application source and third-party libraries;
- certificates, protocols, TLS endpoints, and service meshes;
- cloud accounts, KMS/HSM integrations, keystores, and key ceremonies;
- databases, backups, containers, firmware, devices, telecom, and payment
  infrastructure;
- suppliers, vendors, subprocessors, and long-lived products;
- policies, contracts, exception records, and migration projects.

A security or architecture team may therefore know that cryptography matters but
still lack a reliable answer to:

- Where is a vulnerable or non-approved algorithm actually used?
- Which business service, supplier, key, certificate, library, or device depends
  on it?
- What can be changed safely, in what order, and by whom?
- Is a PQC statement an observed fact, a vendor assertion, a planned change, or
  an unknown?
- Can the organization show the evidence and review history behind a decision?
- Did a cryptographic configuration change create a new attack path or break an
  existing control?

CISA's PQC material explicitly connects migration planning with cryptographic
inventory, supply-chain assessment, and vendor engagement. NIST CSF 2.0 also
places governance and supply-chain risk management in the core framework. Those
are strong problem signals, but they do not approve CryptoScan or guarantee a
purchase.

## 3. CryptoScan's addressable wedge

CryptoScan should be described as a **cryptographic threat-intelligence and
crypto-agility evidence platform**. The product wedge is the connected workflow:

```text
observe
  -> normalize cryptographic facts
  -> produce CBOM and related inventories
  -> map assets to systems, owners, vendors, and business impact
  -> classify policy, lifecycle, quantum, and threat exposure
  -> prioritize migration and remediation
  -> monitor drift and suspicious change
  -> create evidence, reports, and review history
```

### 3.1 What CBOM contributes

CBOM is the **inventory and relationship layer**. It answers:

- which algorithms, protocols, certificates, keys, libraries, and cryptographic
  assets are observed;
- where the observation came from;
- which components use or provide the asset;
- what is known, unknown, unverified, or incomplete;
- what must be rechecked after a code, configuration, vendor, or infrastructure
  change.

CBOM is not the complete product and is not itself a threat verdict. OWASP
CycloneDX describes CBOM as one of the BOM types in the CycloneDX/ECMA-424
family. CryptoScan's differentiation should be the intelligence, context,
prioritization, and evidence built around the inventory.

Source: [OWASP CycloneDX / ECMA-424](https://owasp.org/projects/cyclonedx)

### 3.2 What PQC contributes

PQC is the **quantum-resilience and migration-planning layer**. It helps answer:

- which public-key uses are potentially quantum-vulnerable;
- which systems have long confidentiality or replacement lifetimes;
- which vendors and products have a known, planned, observed, or unknown PQC
  position;
- where algorithm, library, protocol, certificate, HSM, or firmware changes may
  affect dependencies;
- which migration work is blocked by missing inventory or missing evidence.

NIST approved FIPS 203, FIPS 204, and FIPS 205 in August 2024. Standards being
final does not mean that every library, HSM, device, vendor, or customer estate
is ready. CryptoScan must continue to represent deployment evidence and unknowns
explicitly.

Source: [NIST approved PQC standards](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/08/announcing-approval-three-federal-information-processing-standards-fips)

### 3.3 What the broader Cryptography Threat Intelligence plan contributes

The larger product plan extends beyond CBOM and PQC to answer:

- **What changed?** A new certificate, algorithm, library, protocol, key, vendor
  statement, configuration, or runtime behavior.
- **Why does it matter now?** The change may intersect with a vulnerability,
  exploit technique, active campaign, policy deadline, algorithm deprecation,
  compromised dependency, or business-critical path.
- **What could an attacker do?** A cryptographic misuse may enable downgrade,
  interception, impersonation, unauthorized signing, key abuse, weak
  authentication, data exposure, or persistence. These are hypotheses until
  supported by evidence.
- **What should the defender do?** Validate, contain, rotate, revoke, patch,
  migrate, add a control, open a ticket, or accept the risk with an accountable
  owner and expiry.
- **How do we know it worked?** Re-scan, verify telemetry, compare the CBOM,
  re-run policy evaluation, preserve evidence, and record the review outcome.

That is the strategic category: **cryptography threat intelligence**, not merely
CBOM export and not merely PQC readiness scoring.

## 4. Commercial value to a customer

The product's customer value is created by reducing decision uncertainty and
making cryptographic work executable. The customer may receive value through:

| Value mechanism | Customer question | Evidence required before claiming success |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery efficiency | How much manual inventory work was avoided? | Baseline time, source scope, analyst effort, and validated coverage |
| Faster prioritization | Did the team identify the highest-impact cryptographic risks earlier? | Customer-approved risk model, timestamps, and finding validation |
| Safer migration | Did the team reduce rework or prevent an unsafe sequence? | Migration plan, dependencies, change records, and repeat verification |
| Better supplier decisions | Did vendor evidence become comparable and reviewable? | Vendor records, provenance, reviewer decisions, and unresolved unknowns |
| Lower audit friction | Can the organization show what it observed and decided? | Hashes, evidence references, approval history, and export verification |
| Faster response | Did a cryptographic anomaly receive an actionable response? | Detection timestamp, triage record, containment, and recovery evidence |

CryptoScan must not claim that it prevents breaches, proves compliance, or
creates a particular financial saving without customer-specific evidence.

A defensible customer-value calculation is:

```text
customer value
  = measured analyst time avoided
  + measured delay avoided
  + measured outage/rework cost avoided
  + risk-reduction value accepted by the customer
  + audit/evidence effort avoided
  - CryptoScan fees
```

The risk-reduction term must be a customer-approved scenario, not a fabricated
probability of breach or a guaranteed loss avoided.

## 6. Beachhead and expansion logic

A disciplined market sequence is:

1. **Beachhead:** regulated organizations with a named cryptography, PKI,
   security-architecture, or migration owner.
2. **Proof:** paid or approved pilot using customer-controlled scope and
   customer-reviewed findings.
3. **Expansion:** more repositories, cloud accounts, certificates, networks,
   vendors, HSM/KMS sources, business units, and evidence workflows.
4. **Intelligence:** threat enrichment, change correlation, and cryptographic
   incident detection.
5. **Defence:** controlled response, migration orchestration, policy guardrails,
   and verified recovery.
6. **Ecosystem:** suppliers, integrators, assessors, insurers, and technology
   partners using governed evidence and machine-readable outputs.

The initial buyer may be a CISO, chief architect, PKI/crypto owner, cloud
security lead, risk owner, or vendor-risk function. The economic buyer is not
assumed; it must be identified during discovery.

## 7. What would increase value

The strongest future value signals are measurable:

- validated coverage against a customer reference estate;
- reduction in time to locate and classify cryptographic use;
- high-confidence mapping from crypto asset to owner and business service;
- repeatable PQC migration plans with dependency and rollback evidence;
- useful cryptographic threat detections with low false-positive burden;
- integrations into SIEM, SOAR, ITSM, CI/CD, PKI, HSM/KMS, and vendor-risk
  workflows;
- customer-reviewed evidence packs and successful audit reuse;
- safe operation in customer-controlled or regulated deployment models;
- renewal, expansion, and referenceability.

Until these are measured in real customer environments, they remain product
hypotheses and qualification objectives.

## 8. Boundaries and anti-fabrication rules

CryptoScan should not state that it:

- owns or controls the global PQC or threat-intelligence market;
- is worth a specific valuation without finance and transaction evidence;
- has a specific market share;
- prevents all cryptographic attacks;
- detects every cryptographic threat;
- proves a customer is compliant or production-ready;
- replaces a SOC, SIEM, HSM, KMS, certificate authority, auditor, or regulator;
- treats an unconfigured scanner, missing connector, vendor assertion, or
  inferred algorithm as verified evidence.

The market opportunity can be large while the present company value remains
unproven. The correct path is evidence-led qualification, not a larger claim.
